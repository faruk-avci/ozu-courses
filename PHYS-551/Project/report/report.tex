% ============================ INTRODUCTION ============================
\section{Introduction}

A large electrical grid does not fail one component at a time. When a single
district is pushed past the limit of its substations, its excess load does not
simply vanish; it is rerouted onto the neighbouring districts. If those
neighbours are themselves close to their limit, the overload spreads, and a
local fault grows into a city-wide blackout. This chain reaction is called a
\emph{cascading failure}. Because the number of possible failure combinations in
a grid of tens of districts is enormous, and because the truly catastrophic
events are very rare, deterministic worst-case analysis is not practical. This
term project therefore studies the problem with Monte Carlo simulation.

The grid studied here is the mainland of Istanbul, modelled as 38 districts on
the European and Asian sides. Each district has a population, a base
electricity load, and a safety margin that is calibrated from real overload
records published by EPİAŞ. The central difficulty is that a blackout affecting
millions of people is a \emph{rare event}: under normal operation it almost never
happens, so a naive (crude) Monte Carlo simulation would have to run for an
impractically long time before it ever observed one. The project answers two
questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What is the probability that a cascading failure leaves at least $X$
    people without power on a given day, including the rare tail that naive
    Monte Carlo cannot reach? I answer this with Importance Sampling.
    \item Given the resulting risk map, \emph{where} should new relief
    substations be built to reduce the expected number of people affected the
    most?
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this report is organised as follows. Section~2 describes the
data and the model: the districts, how loads and safety margins are obtained,
and the cascading-failure mechanism. Section~3 presents the naive Monte Carlo
baseline and shows where it breaks down. Section~4 introduces the Importance
Sampling estimator and the systemic-surge bias that makes the rare tail
tractable. Section~5 compares the two methods directly through the exceedance
curve, the variance-reduction table, and a convergence study. Section~6 applies
the model to the substation-placement question. Section~7 concludes.

% ============================ MODEL & DATA ============================
\section{System Model and Data}

\subsection{Districts, Population, and Load}

The grid is represented as a graph of 38 mainland districts (the islands are
excluded), with a total population of about \num{15736564}. Two districts are
connected by an edge if they share a physical border, giving 83 edges; the
adjacency was extracted from district geometry and adjusted for the Bosphorus
bridge crossings. Figure~\ref{fig:graph} shows the resulting network.

Each district is assigned a base electricity load $\mu_i$. The total monthly
consumption of the European and Asian distribution regions (from EPİAŞ
consumption data) is distributed among the districts of each side in proportion
to their population:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_i = C_{\text{side}} \cdot \frac{\text{Pop}_i}{\sum_{j \in \text{side}} \text{Pop}_j},
\end{equation}
where $C_{\text{side}}$ is the average monthly consumption of the side that
district $i$ belongs to. This makes the load distribution reflect the real demand
split between the two sides of the city. Because both capacity and demand are
proportional to $\mu_i$ (Section~2.3), the model is scale-free: only dimensionless
ratios enter the results, so the absolute size of $\mu_i$ and its time unit are
immaterial.

    \caption{The Istanbul mainland grid as a graph of 38 districts. Nodes are
    placed at district centroids; edges connect bordering districts.}

\subsection{Safety Margins from Real Overload Data}

Each district is given a dimensionless safety margin $\alpha_i$, defined so that
its capacity is
\begin{equation}
    \text{capacity}_i = \mu_i \, (1 + \alpha_i).
\end{equation}
A district with a large $\alpha_i$ can absorb a big demand spike before tripping;
a district with a small $\alpha_i$ is fragile. Rather than assign these margins
arbitrarily, they are calibrated from one year of EPİAŞ outage records by
counting, for each district, how many outages were caused by overload
(``Aşırı Yük''). The margins are then assigned \emph{per side} (because the two
sides are operated by different distribution companies) by linearly mapping the
overload count to the range $[\alpha_{\min}, \alpha_{\max}] = [0.08, 0.25]$:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i = \alpha_{\max} - \frac{n_i}{\max_{j \in \text{side}} n_j}\,(\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min}),
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the overload count of district $i$. The districts with the most
recorded overloads are therefore given the smallest margins. For example,
Maltepe (465 overloads, the worst on the Asian side) and Fatih and Beyoğlu (108
each, the worst on the European side) receive $\alpha = 0.08$, while a quiet
district such as Çatalca (2 overloads) is close to $\alpha = 0.25$. This is what
makes the model data-driven: the historically weakest districts are the
structurally weakest in the simulation.

\subsection{The Cascading-Failure Mechanism}

\paragraph{Daily demand.}
Electricity demand fluctuates from day to day. The demand of district $i$ on a
given day is modelled as
\begin{equation}
    L_i = \mu_i \, (S + \varepsilon_i),
\end{equation}
where $S \sim \mathcal{N}(1, \sigma_{\text{sys}}^2)$ is a
city-wide demand multiplier shared by every district, and
$\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{idio}}^2)$ is a small
district-specific fluctuation. The systemic term $S$ represents a common cause,
such as a heatwave that turns on every air conditioner in the city at once; it is
the reason failures are correlated, and a rare large value of $S$ is what drives
a city-wide cascade. I use $\sigma_{\text{sys}} = 0.045$ and
$\sigma_{\text{idio}} = 0.02$.

\paragraph{Tripping and redistribution.}
A district trips when its demand exceeds its capacity, i.e.\ when
$L_i > \text{capacity}_i$. This breaker is a hard threshold, so once the demand
vector is drawn the whole cascade is deterministic; all randomness lives in $S$
and the $\varepsilon_i$. When district $i$ trips, a fraction of its excess load is
pushed onto its still-active neighbours:
\begin{equation}
    \text{load to move} = \gamma_i \,(L_i - \text{capacity}_i),
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_i \in (0,1]$ is a redistribution factor. The load is split among
the live neighbours in proportion to each neighbour's remaining headroom, so
load preferentially flows to the districts most able to absorb it. A neighbour
that is itself pushed over its capacity then trips in turn, and the process
repeats until no further districts fail. The redistribution factor is kept
deliberately moderate ($\gamma_i \le 0.35$) so that a single trip does not
automatically collapse the whole city; a truly large blackout requires a large
systemic surge $S$, which is rare. The quantity of interest at the end of each
simulated day is the total population without power, i.e.\ the sum of
the populations of all tripped districts.

% ============================ NAIVE MC ============================
\section{Naive Monte Carlo Baseline}

The naive (crude) estimator samples the model exactly as described above:
for each of $N$ simulated days, a systemic surge $S$ and the district
fluctuations $\varepsilon_i$ are drawn from their true distributions, the cascade
is run, and the affected population is recorded. The probability that at least
$X$ people lose power is estimated by the fraction of days on which this
happened,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{P}(\text{affected} \ge X) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\{\text{affected}_k \ge X\},
\end{equation}
with the usual binomial standard error $\sqrt{\hat{P}(1-\hat{P})/N}$.

Table~\ref{tab:naive} shows the result of a run with $N = \num{100000}$ days.
The estimator is accurate for the common, small events: outages affecting more
than \num{100000} people occur on about $9\%$ of days, and the curve is
well-resolved down to the \num{2000000}-person level (277 observed events). But
the deep tail reveals the central problem: in \num{100000} simulated days, only
two events affecting more than \num{8000000} people were observed. Two
events give a hopelessly noisy estimate (a relative error near $70\%$), and a
slightly rarer event would not have been seen at all. The naive method cannot
reliably reach the events that matter most.

% ============================ IMPORTANCE SAMPLING ============================
\section{Importance Sampling}

\subsection{Idea and Estimator}

Importance Sampling removes the blindness of the naive method by drawing samples
from a different, biased distribution $g$ that makes the rare event common, and
then correcting for the bias with a weight. If the true distribution is $f$ and
I instead sample from a proposal $g$, the unbiased estimator of any
probability is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{P} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\{\text{event}_k\}\, W_k,
    \qquad W_k = \frac{f(x_k)}{g(x_k)} .
\end{equation}
The weight $W_k$ compensates for the fact that the biased distribution visits the
rare region more often than reality would.

\subsection{Biasing the Systemic Surge}

The key modelling insight is that, in this grid, almost all of the tail risk is
driven by the single systemic variable $S$: a city-wide blackout happens
essentially only when $S$ is large. I therefore bias \emph{only} $S$, shifting
its mean upward by $\theta$ standard deviations,
\begin{equation}
    g:\quad S \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(1 + \theta\,\sigma_{\text{sys}},\; \sigma_{\text{sys}}^2\right),
\end{equation}
while the district fluctuations $\varepsilon_i$ are left untouched, and the cascade
itself is deterministic given the demand, so neither needs reweighting. Because the
bias acts on a single dimension, the likelihood ratio is just a ratio of two
Gaussian densities and collapses to a closed form. Writing
$z_S = (S-1)/\sigma_{\text{sys}}$,
\begin{equation}
    W = \frac{f(S)}{g(S)}
      = \frac{\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,z_S^{2}\right)}
             {\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(z_S-\theta)^{2}\right)}
      = \exp\!\left(-\theta\,z_S + \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^{2}\right),
    \qquad \ln W = -\theta\,z_S + \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^{2}.
\end{equation}
The weight depends on the draw only through the single number $z_S$, so it stays
well-behaved. This is far more stable than biasing all 38 districts independently,
where the weight would be a product of 38 such factors and would degenerate.

\paragraph{Choosing the tilt.} For estimating a Gaussian-tail probability
$P(S > c)$, the variance-minimising exponential tilt shifts the proposal mean onto
the threshold itself, $\theta^{\star} \approx (c-1)/\sigma_{\text{sys}}$ -- the
$z$-score of the event. A more severe blackout corresponds to a larger surge $c$,
hence a larger optimal $\theta$; this is why no single tilt is best for the whole
curve and a ladder of tilts is needed.

A single value of $\theta$ samples only one part of the tail well, so I run a
\emph{ladder} of tilts $\theta \in \{2.0$, $2.5$, $3.0$, $3.5$, $4.0\}$ with \num{80000}
samples each and combine them with \emph{multiple importance sampling} (the
balance heuristic): every draw, whichever tilt produced it, is reweighted by
\begin{equation}
    w(S) = \frac{f(S)}{\bar{g}(S)}, \qquad
    \bar{g}(S) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{l=1}^{K} g_l(S),
\end{equation}
the ratio of the true density to the equal mixture of the $K=5$ proposals. All
$KN = \num{400000}$ draws are pooled into one unbiased estimator
$\hat{P}(\ge t) = \tfrac{1}{KN}\sum \mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\, w$. This avoids the bias
of selecting a ``best'' tilt per threshold and yields one honest effective sample
size for the whole pool. The rarest bucket ($\ge \num{8000000}$ people), which the
naive method barely saw, is estimated as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{P}(\text{affected} \ge \num{8000000}) = (5.22 \pm 0.07)\times 10^{-6},
\end{equation}
a one-in-\num{191000}-day event, with a relative error near $1\%$. The pooled
effective sample size is modest (about $0.4\%$ of the draws), but that global
figure is not the right yardstick here: because the high tilts place their samples
exactly on the rare region, the \emph{per-threshold} relative error is what
matters, and it stays around $1\%$ across the tail (Table~\ref{tab:vr}).

\subsection{A Closed-Form Check}

Before trusting the simulated probabilities, it is worth checking the model
against a hand calculation. Ignoring the cascade, the most fragile districts
(Maltepe, Beyoğlu, Fatih, with $\alpha = 0.08$) fail exactly when their own demand
exceeds capacity, i.e.\ when $S + \varepsilon_i > 1 + \alpha$. Since
$S + \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(1, \sigma_{\text{tot}}^2)$ with
$\sigma_{\text{tot}} = \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{sys}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{idio}}^2}
= \sqrt{0.045^2 + 0.02^2} \approx 0.049$, the direct-overload probability is
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{direct}} = \Phi\!\left(-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma_{\text{tot}}}\right)
    = \Phi(-1.63) \approx 5.2\%.
\end{equation}
The simulation gives $5.2\%$ for these districts -- essentially identical, which
validates it against an independent calculation. The match also shows that the
cascade adds almost nothing to a single fragile district's \emph{own} probability:
when such a district trips, its excess is absorbed by well-margined neighbours
rather than tipping them. The cascade's real effect is instead to make failures
\emph{correlated}, and it is that correlation -- many districts going down on the
same large-$S$ day -- that populates the deep tail of the population curve.

\subsection{The Exceedance Curve}

Figure~\ref{fig:ep} overlays the two estimators. On the common events (left side

\subsection{Variance Reduction}

Table~\ref{tab:vr} quantifies the gain. The variance-reduction factor is the
ratio of the per-sample variances of the two estimators, evaluated at the true
probability. For common events the gain is negligible (the two estimators are
essentially equivalent there), but it grows steeply as the event becomes rarer,
reaching roughly $10\,000\times$ for the rarest bucket. The naive figure in the

column states the gain in the most intuitive way: to expect even a \emph{single}
$\ge\num{8000000}$ event, the naive method needs on the order of \num{190000}
simulated days, whereas Importance Sampling resolves its probability to about
$1\%$ relative error from a pooled \num{400000} tilted draws.

\subsection{Convergence}

Figure~\ref{fig:conv} tracks the running estimate of the rarest bucket against
the number of samples. The Importance Sampling estimate settles smoothly onto its
value of about $5.2\times 10^{-6}$ with a steadily shrinking confidence band. The
naive estimate, by contrast, sits at zero for the first $\sim$\num{39000} days,
then jumps discontinuously each time it happens to record one of its two events
and drifts at roughly $2\times10^{-5}$ -- about four times too high -- with an
error band that still spans zero at $N=\num{100000}$. The naive curve is therefore
not converging to anything useful; it is just sampling noise. This is a direct
picture of why the rare-event problem requires variance reduction rather than
simply more samples.

Combining the per-district failure probabilities with the populations gives the

% ============================ STATION PLACEMENT ============================
\section{Application: Siting New Relief Substations}

\subsection{Model of a New Station}

The risk map suggests an obvious engineering question: if two new relief
substations can be built, where should they go to protect the most people? A new
station is modelled as a node that connects to at most three districts and
\emph{takes over $30\%$ of the demand} of each district it serves. Because the
relieved districts keep their original physical capacity but now carry less load,
they sit further below their tripping limit and fail far less often. The new
station is given a healthy average margin ($\alpha = 0.20$), carries no
population of its own, and also acts as a high-headroom sink that can absorb
cascade spillover from its neighbours.

The objective is to minimise the expected daily population without power. Since
this expectation is dominated by the common single-district trips, it can be
evaluated quickly with the naive estimator. The two placements are chosen
\emph{greedily}: candidate stations are formed around the highest-risk districts
and their neighbours, the candidate giving the largest reduction is fixed, and
the procedure is repeated for the second station.

\subsection{Result}

The optimiser independently selected the two high-risk clusters identified in
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}: one station serving Beyoğlu, Fatih, and Şişli on the
European side, and one serving Kartal, Maltepe, and Pendik on the Asian side. The
effect is large, as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:place}: the expected daily
population without power falls from about \num{68000} to about \num{4100}, a
reduction of roughly $94\%$. The rare tail is suppressed as well -- for instance
the probability of a $\ge \num{1000000}$-person event drops by a factor of about
$43$. Figure~\ref{fig:after} shows the risk map after the two stations are added
(almost entirely green), and Figure~\ref{fig:placeep} shows the before/after
exceedance curves.

% ============================ LIMITATIONS ============================
\section{Limitations and Assumptions}

The model is intentionally simplified, and its absolute probabilities should be
read as illustrative rather than predictive. The main assumptions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No power-flow physics. The grid is a graph with a load-shedding
    rule, not a true electrical model: there are no line impedances, no Kirchhoff
    laws, and no voltage or frequency dynamics. The adjacency is \emph{geographic}
    (which districts border each other), which is only a proxy for the real
    electrical transmission topology.
    \item Demand model. Daily demand is Gaussian about a population-scaled
    mean. Real demand has daily and seasonal cycles and heavier tails than a normal
    distribution.
    \item Calibration. District loads are split by population, and safety
    margins are mapped from overload counts by a linear heuristic; neither uses
    confidential utility capacity data.
    \item Cascade rule. Redistribution is a simple headroom-weighted split
    with a sharp breaker threshold. Real cascades involve protection relays,
    controlled islanding, and generator dynamics.
\end{itemize}

Because of these choices, the value of the work lies in the \emph{methodology}
(rare-event estimation by importance sampling) and in the \emph{relative}
conclusions (which districts dominate the risk, and where reinforcement helps
most), rather than in the precise numerical probabilities. Most of these
limitations are also natural directions for making the model more realistic.

% ============================ CONCLUSION ============================
\section{Conclusion}

This project modelled cascading blackouts in the Istanbul mainland grid as a
Monte Carlo problem, with district safety margins calibrated from real EPİAŞ
overload data. The central finding is methodological: a city-wide blackout is a
genuinely rare event, and naive Monte Carlo cannot resolve it -- in \num{100000}
simulated days it logged only two events affecting more than 8 million people, an
estimate so dominated by sampling noise that its $95\%$ interval runs from near
zero to several times the true value. By biasing a single systemic demand
variable and reweighting, Importance Sampling estimated the same probability as
$(5.22 \pm 0.07)\times 10^{-6}$, with a variance reduction of up to about
$10\,000\times$, while agreeing with the naive method on the common events that
validate it. Finally, the risk map showed that vulnerability is concentrated in a
few historically weak districts, and a greedy placement of two relief substations
on those clusters cut the expected affected population by about $94\%$. The work
demonstrates both why variance reduction is necessary for rare-event risk and how
the resulting estimates can directly inform an engineering decision.
